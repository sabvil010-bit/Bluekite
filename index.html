Moon phases explained

Half the Moon is always lit. We just see it from different angles.

The Sun always lights the half of the Moon facing it. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see more or less of that sunlit half. One full cycle, from new moon back to new moon, takes about 29.5 days.

  • Waxing means the lit part we can see is growing; waning means it is shrinking.
  • Phases are not caused by Earth's shadow. That only happens during a lunar eclipse.
  • Shapes below are as seen from the Northern Hemisphere. In the Southern Hemisphere, they appear flipped left to right.

Short lessons

Big questions, short answers.

Each lesson explains one idea in plain words, with a quick experiment you can try at home or in class.

5 min readAges 10+

Why do we have seasons?

Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5°. As Earth travels around the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans towards the Sun, getting more direct sunlight and longer days — that's summer. It isn't about distance: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter.

Try it: shine a torch straight down on paper, then at a slant. Which patch of light is brighter?

6 min readAges 11+

How gravity keeps orbits

Imagine throwing a ball so fast that, as it falls, the ground curves away beneath it just as quickly. That's an orbit: the Moon and planets are always falling, but their sideways speed means they never hit what they're orbiting. Isaac Newton explained this with a thought experiment about a cannon on a very tall mountain.

Try it: swing a soft ball on a string in a circle. The string plays the part of gravity.

4 min readAges 10+

What is a light-year?

A light-year is a distance, not a time: how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion km. Light is the fastest thing there is, yet it still takes over four years to reach us from Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun. When you look at the stars, you are seeing light that left them long ago.

Try it: sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth. How far away is the Sun in “light-minutes”?

5 min readAges 10+

What causes the Moon's phases?

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half — none at new moon, all of it at full moon. The whole cycle takes about 29.5 days, and it has nothing to do with Earth's shadow.

Try it: in a dark room, hold a ball at arm's length near a lamp and turn slowly on the spot.

3 min readAges 10+

Why is Mars red?

Mars's surface is covered in dust and rocks rich in iron oxide — the same compound that makes rust. Fine dust blown high into its thin atmosphere gives the daytime sky a butterscotch colour. Strangely, sunsets on Mars look bluish.

Try it: leave a steel wool pad damp for a few days (with an adult's help) and watch it turn reddish-brown.

5 min readAges 12+

What makes the tides?

The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, raising a bulge of water on the side facing the Moon and another on the opposite side. As Earth spins through these bulges, most coasts get two high tides roughly every 24 hours and 50 minutes. At new and full moon, the Sun's pull lines up with the Moon's, giving extra-large “spring tides”.

Try it: check a local tide table and see how high tide moves about 50 minutes later each day.

Glossary

Words worth knowing.

Space science has its own vocabulary. Here are the terms used on this site, in plain language.

Aphelion
The point in an orbit where a planet is farthest from the Sun. Earth reaches aphelion in early July, about 152 million km away.
Asteroid
A small rocky or metallic body orbiting the Sun. Most are found in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
Astronomical unit (AU)
The average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 150 million km. Useful for measuring distances within the solar system.
Axis
An imaginary line through a planet's centre that it spins around. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°.
Comet
A lump of ice, dust and rock. Near the Sun its ice turns to gas, forming a glowing cloud and one or more tails.
Dwarf planet
A round body that orbits the Sun but has not cleared other objects from its path. Pluto and Ceres are examples.
Eclipse
When one object passes into the shadow of another. In a lunar eclipse the Moon moves through Earth's shadow; in a solar eclipse the Moon blocks the Sun from part of Earth.
Gravity
The force that pulls objects with mass towards each other. It keeps your feet on the ground and the Moon in orbit.
Light-year
The distance light travels in one year — about 9.46 trillion km. It measures distance, not time.
Orbit
The curved path one object follows around another because of gravity, like Earth around the Sun.
Perihelion
The point in an orbit where a planet is closest to the Sun. Earth reaches perihelion in early January, about 147 million km away.
Satellite
Any object that orbits a planet. The Moon is a natural satellite; the International Space Station is an artificial one.
Solar system
The Sun and everything held by its gravity: eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets.
Terminator
The line between day and night on a planet or moon. On the Moon, it's the edge between the lit and dark parts.
